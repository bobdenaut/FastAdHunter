# 0.2.9 on-device soak — final

**Capture: `2026-08-01T09:17:21Z`. Process uptime 28 h 26 m** (start
`2026-07-31T04:50:50Z`). Raw evidence in [`soak-0.2.9/`](soak-0.2.9/) — every
figure below is reproducible from those files.

- **Image:** `fastadhunter:0.2.9`, image-id `08a8f0b56a18…3a088` — unchanged
  from the checkpoint, so this is one continuous process.
- **Container:** `fastadhunter` on `veth1` / 172.17.0.2, mounts `/config` +
  `/data`, `memory-high=unlimited`, `cpu-list=cpu0..cpu3`.
- **Router:** RB5009, RouterOS 7.21.5, uptime 2 d 23 h 41 m — **not rebooted**
  at any point in the soak.

Everything was read live over HTTPS and read-only SSH. **No writes were made to
the router.**

## Verdict

| Question | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| **Stability** | **PASS.** One start, zero restarts, zero OOM, zero dropped events, zero refresh failures, zero interface drops, 28 h 26 m continuous. |
| **Allocator (mimalloc)** | **PASS.** RSS trend flat-to-negative over the window; committed high-water rose only because a 15-list recompile happened, and the spike was fully reclaimed in 95 min. Zero major faults. |
| **Ship 0.2.9?** | **Yes.** No defect found. Two follow-ups below are design questions, not blockers. |

Two caveats stated up front, because they bound the claim:

1. **This is a 28 h 26 m soak from process start, not the planned
   anchor-to-anchor 24 h window.** That window closes tonight at
   `2026-08-01T19:25:00Z` — 10 h after this capture. Total uptime already
   exceeds 24 h, and the every-60 s series has **no gap larger than 60 s**, so
   the leak question is answered; what tonight would add is a diurnally matched
   comparison, not new information about stability.
2. **The RB5009 was reporting `cpu-frequency: 350 MHz` at 1 % load.** Noted here
   at the time as a possible 4× downclock making every microsecond figure
   pessimistic. **That inference was later shown to be unsafe** — a run
   reporting 1400 MHz performed the same on a control arm
   (`docs/code-review/phase2/p2-10-url-substring-index.md`), so the field does not
   describe what a workload gets. Report it as a condition; calibrate with the
   measured ~9× x86 → RB5009 factor instead.

## Memory — the leak signal

1,270 samples at 60 s, no gap over 60 s. Series in
[`soak-0.2.9/perf-series.csv`](soak-0.2.9/perf-series.csv).

| Figure | Soak window (13.85 h) | Full uptime (28.43 h) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RSS range | 46.5 – 63.8 MB | 38.9 – 63.8 MB |
| RSS mean / p50 | 51.50 / 50.7 MB | 51.25 / 51.6 MB |
| **half-to-half drift** | **+0.28 MB** | **+0.38 MB** |
| **OLS slope** | **−0.131 MB/h (−3.14 MB/day)** | **+0.033 MB/h (+0.80 MB/day)** |
| quarter means (MB) | 53.78 → 48.94 → 51.86 → 51.43 | 50.54 → 51.57 → 51.25 → 51.63 |

The checkpoint set the acceptance bar at "anything under ~2 MB of half-to-half
drift is noise". Both windows land under **0.4 MB**, and the window's regression
slope is *negative*. The quarter means are non-monotone in both cases — RSS
moves, but not in one direction.

**A trap worth naming, because the point-in-time numbers walk straight into
it.** Comparing the two capture instants gives `rss +3.65 MB` and
`residual_bytes +3.55 MB`, which reads like growth. It is not: both are single
samples inside a 17 MB band, and the fitted trend over the same interval is
*downward*. Point-in-time deltas do not measure a leak; the series does.

| Component | Bytes | vs checkpoint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ruleset_bytes` | 23,440,198 | −134,981 (lists shrank on refresh) |
| `cache_estimated_bytes` | 1,445,728 | +386,256 |

## Allocator validation

| Counter | Checkpoint | Capture | Δ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `allocator_committed_bytes` | 249,823,232 | 336,527,360 | **+82.7 MB** |
| `allocator_committed_peak_bytes` | 249,823,232 | 336,527,360 | identical to current |
| `process_peak_rss` | 147,558,400 | 147,841,024 | **+0.27 MB** |
| `major_page_faults` | 0 | **0** | 0 |
| `minor_page_faults` | 82,964 | 231,650 | +148,686 |

**The +82.7 MB commit growth is not retention, and the code already says why.**
`allocator_committed_bytes` is a **lifetime high-water mark** — mimalloc v3 does
not account purges, which is exactly why `peak == current` at every single
reading (documented at `crates/fah-api/src/wire.rs:818-828`). The proof that it
is not resident: RSS sat at 51 MB while the counter claimed 336 MB, and
`process_peak_rss` — a real kernel high-water from `getrusage` — moved
**0.27 MB in 14 hours**. The process never came near its boot spike again.

What raised the water mark is identifiable: the 15-list simultaneous recompile
at 19:59:58Z committed more arena than the boot compile-from-cache had. That
event is visible in RSS and fully reclaimed (next section).

**Purge behaviour under load.** Minor faults rose to 2.98/s, and this is the
useful measurement, since load changed underneath it:

| | checkpoint (0.079 qps) | window (0.561 qps) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| minor faults / s | 1.59 | 2.98 |
| **minor faults / query** | **20.0** | **5.3** |

Per-query fault cost fell ~4× as traffic rose 7×. The purge/recommit cycle
under `MIMALLOC_PURGE_DELAY=100` is therefore **time-driven, not
traffic-driven** — a fixed background cost that amortises away under load
rather than a per-request tax that would scale into the throughput path. Zero
major faults means none of it touched swap.

`crates/fah-api/src/routes.rs:141-143` prescribes reading exactly this pair:
"a rising fault rate at flat RSS is purge thrash, not a leak." Rate rose, RSS
flat, per-query cost fell. Purge thrash, and it is getting cheaper, not worse.

**Verdict on the allocator: mimalloc is behaving as specified.** The +27 %
throughput / −17 % CPU-per-query trade that justified keeping it is not being
paid back in memory drift.

## The recompile spike — measured and fully reclaimed

At `2026-07-31T19:59:58Z` all 15 remaining lists came due at once and
recompiled in a single pass (`rules=697396`, `ruleset_compile_duration_seconds`
= 2.358853, 0 failures, 13 parse errors across 702 k rules).

```text
19:55:52Z  49.6 MB      ← before
20:00:52Z  63.8 MB      ← +14.2 MB, window maximum
20:10:52Z  57.5 MB
21:00:52Z  57.6 MB
21:35:52Z  50.4 MB      ← back to baseline, 95 min after
22:00:52Z  48.5 MB
```

A 15-list recompile costs **+14.2 MB transient**, and mimalloc returns all of
it inside 95 minutes. This is the entire explanation for the window's 17 MB RSS
band — the band is one identified event plus purge oscillation, not drift.

Boot behaviour also held for a second day: at `2026-08-01T04:50:55Z` exactly
**one** list (`spy`) was due and refreshed. The refresh-clock seeding fix that
killed the double compile is still working 24 h in.

## Cache cleanup — the sweeper went live, and now has a cost model

The checkpoint listed this as open item #1: `cache_cleanup_bytes_freed_total`
had never been non-zero, and the earliest anything could be swept was
~`2026-08-01T04:55Z`.

**First real removal: `2026-08-01T04:56:52Z`** — within two minutes of the
predicted floor.

| Counter | Checkpoint | Capture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `cache_cleanup_runs_total` | 145 | 284 |
| `cache_cleanup_entries_removed_total` | 0 | **240** |
| `cache_cleanup_bytes_freed_total` | 0 | **181,376** |
| `cache_cleanup_duration_seconds` (last) | 0.00016 | 0.000505 |

29 non-empty sweeps are logged. Fitting them
([`soak-0.2.9/container-log.txt`](soak-0.2.9/container-log.txt)):

```text
duration_us ≈ 330 + 64.4 × entries_removed      (R² = 0.928, n = 29)
range: 149 – 5,580 µs, over 900 – 1,100 entries walked
```

So the sweep is **dominated by removals, not by the walk** — the walk of ~1,000
entries is the 330 µs intercept; each removed entry adds ~64 µs. The mechanism
is in `DnsCache::clean` (`crates/fah-dns/src/cache.rs:794-801`): any shard that
lost an entry also pays `Shard::sweep_queue`, a second O(len) pass doing one
hash lookup per queue node. With 16 shards, more removals recruit more shards
into that second pass.

**This does not yet settle the deferred `map.shrink_to_fit()` question, and it
is worth being precise about why.** The tables never grew: `table_bytes` counts
`map.capacity()`, and at ~60 entries per shard the maps are nowhere near the
3,125/shard the `max_entries = 50,000` cap allows. The shrink question is
"does a table that ballooned during a burst stay ballooned after the burst
drains?" — and this cache peaked at 1,100 entries, 2.2 % of cap. It needs the
deferred load test: fill, drain, then read `estimated_bytes`. What the soak
*did* deliver is the per-removal cost that a shrink would have to beat.

## Cache

959 – 963 entries (peak 1,100 over the window) of 50,000 — **1.9 % of the entry
cap**, 1.52 % of the byte cap. 9,261 hits / 1,240 misses / **0 evictions**.
Rolling 24 h: 31,210 queries, 22,688 blocked (**72.7 %**), 25.3 % cache-hit.

**One accounting discrepancy, checked and explained.** Three endpoints report
cache bytes and two of them disagree by a stable 42 %:

```text
debug/memory.cache_estimated_bytes  1,450,032   (963 entries)
api/v1/cache.bytes                  1,024,000
metrics component="cache"           1,024,000
```

Held over six back-to-back polls
([`soak-0.2.9/cache-crosscheck.txt`](soak-0.2.9/cache-crosscheck.txt)) — stable,
not a race. It is **by design** and documented at
`crates/fah-dns/src/cache.rs:209-223`: `bytes` is entry heap only, which is the
figure `max_bytes` bounds; `estimated_bytes` additionally counts the hash-table
slabs and the eviction-queue rings, to make the gap to RSS explainable.

The operational consequence is worth writing down anyway: **the configured
`max_bytes` governs entry heap, and true footprint ran 1.42× that.** At the
64 MiB cap the real cost would be nearer 91 MiB. Not a defect — but if
PERFORMANCE.md's 128 MB budget is ever tested against `max_bytes`, that is the
multiplier to apply.

## What this proves and does not prove

**Proves, at up to 0.56 qps sustained over 28 h:** no leak (drift < 0.4 MB
half-to-half, negative slope over the window), no restart, no OOM, no dropped
events, no failed refreshes, no evictions, no interface drops, correct
single-compile boot on a second consecutive day, correct SWR + cleanup
interaction, a fully reclaimed 14 MB recompile spike, and sub-250 µs block p99
on a possibly-downclocked core.

**Does not prove:** anything at the 20 k QPS ceiling. Cache eviction, the byte
cap and the SWR pool's saturation path are still untested — the cache never
exceeded 2.2 % of either bound. Per `feedback-scope-claims-to-evidence`: the
evidence supports *"0.2.9 does not leak at household load up to 0.56 qps"*, not
*"0.2.9 does not leak"*.

## Follow-ups (neither blocks 0.2.9)

1. **`shrink_to_fit` still undecided.** Needs fill-then-drain, not a soak. The
   sweeper's cost to beat is now known: ~330 µs walk + ~64 µs per removal at
   ~1,000 entries.
2. **`sweep_queue` costs ~64 µs per removed entry** — ~90 k cycles at 1.4 GHz,
   more than the mechanism (one hash lookup per queue node) obviously accounts
   for. Not urgent at 240 removals/day; it would matter at cap occupancy.
   Worth a targeted measurement before any cache work in Phase 2/3.
3. **The optional 19:25Z reading.** Available tonight for a diurnally matched
   24 h window. Nothing here suggests it changes the verdict; take it only if a
   like-for-like comparison against the checkpoint is wanted.

Stress testing (`test_aleator.py` or equivalent) is now unblocked — the reading
this window existed to protect has been taken.
